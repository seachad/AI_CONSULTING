Organisation and operating model

Study or report · Accenture · 6 October 2026

When everyone has AI, the advantage lies in the operating model, not in the technology

When everyone has AI, your operating model will set you apart

Article by the consultancy Accenture, published in October 2026. It argues that when every company has access to AI, advantage no longer comes from the technology but from the operating model, meaning how work is organised.

It proposes five shifts: design work around key decisions rather than tasks; organise it in end-to-end value streams tied to customer outcomes; manage people and agents as one workforce, with a named human owner for each agent; measure cost per outcome rather than per employee; and keep a record of decisions.

It is a consultancy publication with a commercial interest.

Study or report · McKinsey & Company · 5 October 2026

Agentic AI changes the three limits that shaped the organisation: capacity, expertise and coordination

McKinsey: AI is changing work. Now it has to change the organization

Article by the consultancy McKinsey, October 2026. It argues that agentic AI changes three limits that shaped the organisation: human capacity, expertise and coordination.

It proposes four shifts. One, organise work around outcomes and draw an "orchestration chart" of people and agents instead of an organisation chart.

Two, rebuild the talent system: decide where freed capacity goes and how beginners learn when AI does the entry-level work. Three, turn AI use into sustained change, with trust. Four, give one leader real authority and create a stable team that redesigns, builds and controls at the same time.

Its figures come from its own surveys.

Study or report · McKinsey & Company · 9 September 2026

AI value depends on the operating model, and a coherent set of well-executed choices wins

McKinsey: The key to AI value is hiding in plain sight: Your operating model

Article by the consultancy McKinsey, September 2026, with a survey of more than 700 executives. It argues that AI value depends on the operating model and that there is no single best model: what wins is a coherent set of choices, well executed.

It describes twelve elements, from structure to rewards. The most advanced organisations organise around end-to-end outcomes, move resources often, standardise their processes, decide fast, manage talent by skills and reward team results.

It warns that automating without redesigning gives less clarity and fewer skills. It sums up five lessons.

The data are respondents' answers about their own organisation, and the article has a commercial interest.

Study or report · McKinsey & Company · 8 July 2026

Organisational readiness for AI weighs almost twice as much as the readiness of its people

McKinsey: From adoption to impact: Three horizons of AI transformation

Article by the consultancy McKinsey, July 2026, with a survey of 750 employees and leaders.

It sorts organisations into three horizons by how deeply AI is embedded in daily work: enablement (tools for each employee), automation (existing workflows automated at scale) and reinvention (roles, workflows and operating model redesigned).

Seventy per cent of respondents feel ready to use AI, but only 27 per cent of leaders see their organisation as ready. Only 11 per cent place their organisation in reinvention. It concludes that organisational readiness weighs almost twice as much as personal readiness, and that trust matters in all three horizons.

These are stated opinions, not measured results.

Study or report · Boston Consulting Group · 15 June 2026

Value comes from redesigning whole processes around agents, not from adding assistants to unchanged processes

AI-First Enterprise Operations: Reinventing the Operating System of Work

Publication by the consultancy Boston Consulting Group, June 2026. It argues that many companies have only added assistants to processes that stay the same, which speeds up single tasks but does not change how work is done.

Value, BCG says, comes from redesigning whole processes around AI agents, with one accountable process owner, exceptions planned in the design and clear governance.

It recommends starting from the desired outcome, beginning with one or two high-value areas, building solid data foundations and treating evaluation and monitoring as production infrastructure.

It is a consultancy source with a commercial interest.

Study or report · MIT NANDA · July 2025

Few organisations see a return from generative AI: bespoke systems rarely move from pilot to production

MIT NANDA: The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025

MIT's Project NANDA report, July 2025, with preliminary findings. It argues that, despite heavy spending on generative AI, few organisations see measurable returns.

General-purpose tools are widely adopted, yet bespoke enterprise systems rarely move from pilot to production. The authors blame systems that neither learn nor fit existing workflows, rather than technology or regulation.

They describe widespread unofficial use of personal accounts, better outcomes with external partners than with in-house builds, and stronger savings in back-office functions. Their advice: buy, give line managers decision authority and demand business outcomes.

The evidence is limited: 52 interviews, self-reported results and acknowledged selection bias.

Study or report · Harvard Business School · March 2025

With generative AI, one person reaches quality similar to a pair without AI, in less time

The Cybernetic Teammate: A Field Experiment on Generative AI Reshaping Teamwork and Expertise

Academic study by researchers from Harvard, Wharton and other institutions, published as a working paper in 2025.

It is a field experiment at a consumer goods company with 776 professionals. Each person, or each pair of one commercial and one technical colleague, tackled a real product challenge with or without generative AI.

One person with AI reached roughly the average quality of a pair without AI, in less time. Pairs with AI were more likely to reach the top 10 per cent of ideas. With AI, technical and commercial proposals looked more alike. People who used AI reported more positive emotions.

It is not peer reviewed and measures one task on one day.

People, trust and talent

Study or report · McKinsey & Company · 12 August 2026

An AI transformation depends on trust, and promising that nothing will change ends up destroying it

McKinsey: AI transformations run on trust

Article by the consultancy McKinsey, August 2026. It argues that an AI transformation cannot be deployed only from the top down: it depends on people trying things, saying what does not work and redesigning their work, and that requires trust in the company.

It separates reducing anxiety from building trust: promising that nothing will change calms people for a while and destroys trust when a decision contradicts it.

It proposes four actions: give a clear plan even if incomplete, go and see how work is done, invest in people (continuous training, pathways and fair treatment of leavers) and equip many leaders to sustain the change. One in four middle managers reports anxiety.

It is a publication with a commercial interest.

Publication · Foro Económico Mundial · January 2025

Employers expect 170 million new jobs and 92 million displaced between 2025 and 2030

World Economic Forum: The Future of Jobs Report 2025

World Economic Forum report, January 2025, based on a survey of 1,043 employers in 55 economies and 22 industries, all with 500 employees or more. It records what those employers expect for 2025-2030.

They foresee 170 million new jobs and 92 million displaced. They expect 39 per cent of the core skills of a job to change. Sixty-three per cent say the skills gap is the biggest barrier to transforming their business. Of every 100 workers, 59 will need training.

In response to AI, 77 per cent plan to reskill their workforce and 41 per cent plan to reduce it where tasks are automated.

These are stated expectations, not results, and small companies are not covered.

Customers' AI agents will raise contact volumes and the cost of concessions

When Your Customer Sends an Agent

Article by the consultancy Bain & Company, November 2026. It explains that personal AI agents remove the effort that used to limit demand for customer service: claiming refunds, negotiating bills or cancelling subscriptions becomes easy.

Study or report · Park et al. (Stanford y otras instituciones) · 15 November 2024

Agents built from interviews simulate each person better than those built from demographics alone

LLM Agents Grounded in Self-Reports Enable General-Purpose Simulation of Individuals (arXiv:2411.10109)

Academic study posted on arXiv by a team from Stanford University and other institutions.

The authors held two-hour interviews with 1,052 US adults and built AI agents from those answers to simulate each person. They found these agents reproduce their source person's survey answers far more accurately than agents built from demographic data alone, and nearly as well as people repeat their own answers two weeks later.

It concerns survey answers, not purchasing behaviour. It is an open academic paper, still being revised.

Authority · Autoridad Bancaria Europea (EBA) · 13 June 2018

The provider that issues the customer's credentials applies strong authentication and decides the exemptions

Opinion of the European Banking Authority on the implementation of the RTS on SCA and CSC (EBA-Op-2018-04)

Opinion of the European Banking Authority, June 2018, addressed to national authorities. It clarifies how the technical standards on strong customer authentication and secure communication under the payment services directive apply.

It says that strong customer authentication is applied, and its exemptions are decided, by the provider that issues the customer's credentials. Third-party providers identify themselves to the bank and do not decide exemptions. The bank may contract another provider to perform authentication and share liability with it. Authentication is required for each payment initiation.

The text predates AI agents and does not mention them: it gives guidance on who authenticates and who is liable.

In insurance, AI is used for routine tasks and very little goes to new products

Unlocking AI value in insurance: A leader's guide to AI-enabled transformation

KPMG International report, September 2026, on AI in the insurance sector. It draws on an online discussion with 44 insurance executives from 20 countries and on its own survey.

It argues that there is a gap between ambition and results: most use AI for routine tasks, about half of the budget goes to efficiency and very little to new products. Only 18 per cent say they have a vision shared by the executive team, and 45 per cent leave AI with the technology function alone.

The sample is small and the report has a commercial interest.
